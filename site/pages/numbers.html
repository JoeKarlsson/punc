Over time

Sentences got shorter and semicolons nearly vanished. About 40% of the shorter sentence is punctuation: writers putting a period where they used to put a semicolon.

How this was counted

Each dot is a book at the year it first came out, and the white line is the median for every 25 years. Translations count by the year of the original. The {{ancient}} books from before 1600, Homer and Plato among them, are off the left edge.

The faint dots and the pink line carry on from 1930: each of {{modernBooks}} novels, and their median for each decade, counted from HathiTrust's page counts. Those counts get commas, semicolons, question marks and sentence ends right, so only those charts have it. The numbers in the text are the Gutenberg books alone.

The dotted line on the words per sentence chart counts every semicolon and colon as a sentence end too. Counted that way, {{punctuationShare}} of the fall from {{firstSpan}} to {{lastSpan}} goes away, and by {{lastSpan}} the two lines meet, at {{sentenceTo}} and {{clauseTo}} words. But not every semicolon or colon ends a thought. Two Claude agents coded 100 random ones from 1700s prose, and in the 82 from English originals, 36 of 50 semicolons (72%) and 19 of 32 colons (59%) joined two clauses that could each stand alone. Counting each mark only that often, the paper puts the punctuation share at about 40% (95% interval 24% to 51%), and every other way of counting it lands between 39% and 48%.

The method and its limits are in the paper (PDF) and the write-up. Karolina Rudnicka's 2018 study found the same fall in semicolons in American English, sharpest between 1810 and 1900.

Authors

Can the marks name the author?

The marks alone name the right author for {{hitRate}} of books. Guessing at random would get about {{chance}}.

Take one book away from its author. Average the fingerprints of each author's other books, and guess whichever author's average is closest to the book. For the {{authors}} authors with at least three different works on the shelf, that guess names the right author for {{hits}} of {{guessed}} books. Leave any one mark out of every fingerprint and the test still gets {{withoutLow}} to {{withoutHigh}} right, so no single mark decides it. Leaving out the {{markMost}} costs the most, and the {{markLeast}} the least.

Each square is one of an author's books, oldest first: pink where the guess named the author. Spread is how far an author's books stray from their own average fingerprint, where 100 is the gap between two books picked at random from the shelf. The lower it is, the more alike their books are.

Alexandra Darmon and colleagues (2021) tried this on Project Gutenberg too, with 651 authors and a neural network that learns which mark follows which, and named the right author about 62% of the time. Their model is stronger and their setup different, so the numbers don't compare directly. One of their cautions holds here: a Gutenberg text is punctuated by its editors, printers and translators as well as its author.
