The numbers

Punc took every word out of {{books}} books and counted what was left. Every chart here measures the marks; the year, genre, author and download rank only sort the books into groups. Point at a dot to see its book.

What Punc found

  1. Semicolons nearly vanished. The typical book had {{semicolons}}.
  2. Sentences got shorter, but not by as much as it looks. The typical sentence fell from {{sentenceFrom}} words in {{firstSpan}} to {{sentenceTo}} in {{lastSpan}}. Count every semicolon and colon as the end of a sentence too, and {{punctuationShare}} of that fall goes away: older writers joined their thoughts with ; and : where newer ones put a period.
  3. Genre shows in the marks. {{genreFinding}}
  4. The marks alone can name the author. Punc's test gets {{hitRate}} of books right, where guessing would get {{chance}}, and no single mark gives the author away.
  5. Books centuries apart can punctuate alike. {{twins}} are each other's closest match on the shelf.
  6. Do popular books punctuate differently? {{popularity}}

Over time

Sentences got shorter and semicolons nearly vanished.

Each dot is a book at the year it first came out, and the white line is the median for every 25 years. Translations count by the year of the original. The {{ancient}} books from before 1600, Homer and Plato among them, are off the left edge.

Part of the shorter sentence is a change in punctuation, not in writing. The dotted line on the words per sentence chart counts every semicolon and colon as the end of a sentence too. Older writers joined clauses with them where a newer writer puts a period, and once they count, {{punctuationShare}} of the fall from {{firstSpan}} to {{lastSpan}} goes away. By {{lastSpan}} the two lines meet, at {{sentenceTo}} and {{clauseTo}} words, because books barely use either mark any more. The rest of the fall is sentences really getting shorter.

Linguists have seen both changes. Karolina Rudnicka's study of sentence length (2018) traces the shrinking sentence back to writers who noticed it in the 1890s, finds semicolons falling sharply between 1810 and 1900 in a large corpus of American English, and discusses the argument that shorter sentences are partly a by-product of changing punctuation. The dotted line puts a size on that argument.

That {{punctuationShare}} counts every semicolon and colon as a sentence end, and not all of them are. In 100 random ones from 1700s prose, coded twice by two separate Claude agents that agreed on 95, 72% of the semicolons and 58% of the colons joined two clauses that could each stand alone; the rest separated list items or introduced a quotation. Counting each mark only as often as it really ends something, and checking every other way I could think of, about 40% of the fall is punctuation. The method and the limits are in the write-up.

The white line is the median of every book.

Genres

{{genreFinding}}

Each genre's average fingerprint: every mark's share of all the marks, on the same scale as the fingerprints on the shelf. A book can sit in more than one genre. Plays look short on dialogue only because they don't put speech in quotation marks.

Authors

Can the marks name the author?

The marks alone name the right author for {{hitRate}} of books. Guessing at random would get about {{chance}}.

Take one book away from its author. Average the fingerprints of each author's other books, and guess whichever author's average is closest to the book. For the {{authors}} authors with at least three different works on the shelf, that guess names the right author for {{hits}} of {{guessed}} books. No single mark carries it: leave any one mark out of every fingerprint and the test still gets {{withoutLow}} to {{withoutHigh}} right. The {{markMost}} matters most and the {{markLeast}} least. An author shows in how all the marks sit together.

Each square is one of an author's books, oldest first: pink where the guess named the author. Spread is how far an author's books stray from their own average fingerprint, where 100 is the gap between two books picked at random from the shelf. The lower it is, the more alike their books are.

Alexandra Darmon and colleagues (2021) tried this on Project Gutenberg too, with 651 authors and a neural network that learns which mark follows which, and named the right author about 62% of the time. Their model is stronger and their setup different, so the numbers don't compare directly. One of their cautions holds here: a Gutenberg text is punctuated by its editors, printers and translators as well as its author.
